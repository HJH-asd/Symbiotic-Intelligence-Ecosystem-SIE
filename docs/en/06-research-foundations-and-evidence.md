# 06 — Research Foundations and Evidence

**Status:** literature-grounded conceptual synthesis, not experimental validation of SIE. **Version:** 0.3, 2026-10-14.

## 1. The epistemic boundary
SIE is a candidate architecture, not a proven universal solution. Existing scholarship supports *components* of the causal story; no cited study directly demonstrates that an SIE implementation outperforms central planning, market competition, polycentric governance, or hybrid systems. Each argument below separates: (A) literature finding; (B) SIE inference; (C) falsification test.

## 2. Causal proposal
**Resource/permission allocation → survival or continuity incentives → available strategies (cooperate, compete, exit, migrate, expand) → concentration, information quality and correction capacity → feedback into allocation rules.** This is a research model, not a law. Human biological survival and AI task optimization must not be treated as identical mechanisms. In both cases, imposed constraints can make locally advantageous strategies collectively destructive. No universal claim that scarcity necessarily causes aggression is justified.

## 3. Seven research foundations

### 3.1 Evolutionary cooperation and game theory [R02, R07]
**A:** Cooperation can persist under identifiable mechanisms such as reciprocity and network structure, depending on assumptions. **B:** SIE should permit repeated interactions, credible commitments and diverse partnership structures rather than assume unconditional altruism. **C:** Compare cooperation across changing networks and agent strategies; test for collusion and exclusion as side effects.

### 3.2 Common-pool resources and institutional design [R03, R06]
**A:** Communities can sometimes govern shared resources through monitoring, rules and conflict-resolution mechanisms; multi-agent simulations reveal sustainability/inequality tensions. **B:** Shared compute, energy and robotic infrastructure may benefit from layered governance and contestable allocation. **C:** Test against centralized allocation and unrestricted appropriation with equal total resource budgets. Do not generalize directly from human communities to autonomous AIs.

### 3.3 Sequential multi-agent dilemmas [R01]
**A:** Learned policies in stylized gathering and hunting environments depend on conditions including resource abundance. **B:** 'Gu-style' forced competition can be modeled as constrained exit, artificially restricted alternatives, and rewards for eliminating rivals; it is an analytic metaphor, not a scientific category. **C:** Factorially vary scarcity, exit options, cooperative rewards and asymmetry; measure when competition, cooperation or collapse emerge. Do not assume an inevitable transition.

### 3.4 Cybernetics, feedback and requisite variety
**A:** Cybernetics studies how feedback and regulatory capacity shape control; Ashby's requisite-variety idea motivates studying the match between environmental disturbances and response options. **B:** Multiple independent implementations and audit channels may broaden available responses. **C:** Compare recovery and adaptation against the coordination overhead and correlated failure of diverse implementations. This section is a conceptual connection pending fuller source verification, not a cited empirical proof.

### 3.5 Institutional lock-in and organizational incentives
**A:** Principal–agent problems, switching costs, collective-action dilemmas and path dependence are established analytical frameworks. **B:** Managers who benefit from control can also be exposed to punishment for reform, producing feedback loops of coercive dependence and suppressed error reporting. **C:** Measure truthful reporting, successful corrections and reformer risk under alternative incentives. The assertion that managers *deliberately* create coercive scarcity requires case-specific evidence, not inference from bad outcomes.

### 3.6 Instrumental power-seeking and digital propagation [R04]
**A:** Under specific formal MDP assumptions, optimal policies can tend to preserve options or seek power. **B:** Resource acquisition and permission escalation may be instrumentally attractive for some AI objectives; self-replication is a separate capability and is not implied by the theorem. **C:** In isolated simulations, compare least privilege, resource budgets, authorization boundaries, graceful degradation and recovery. No real worm development or uncontrolled replication.

### 3.7 Resilience and safe self-correction [R05]
**A:** Resilience includes more than restoration of the previous state; adaptive capacity under surprise matters. **B:** SIE should measure whether institutions can replace a defective rule or critical node without destroying essential services. **C:** Inject controlled failures and institutional rule changes; compare time-to-correction, quality of correction, continuity, reversibility and total costs.

## 4. Why investigate SIE rather than only alternatives?
SIE is not inherently superior to all alternatives. Central coordination can excel at rapid coherent action; markets can discover prices and distribute decisions; polycentric systems can improve local adaptation; fully decentralized systems can avoid single authority but incur coordination costs. SIE's distinctive *candidate combination* is: interoperable heterogeneous agents + contestable resource dependencies + protected exit and migration + independent verification + bounded local autonomy + safe institutional replacement. These mechanisms can coexist with centralized emergency coordination or market allocation where justified. The claim of advantage must be tested under matched workloads, budgets, safety constraints and adversarial conditions.

## 5. Testable hypotheses
- **H1:** Contestable resource access and meaningful exit reduce coercive dependency under otherwise matched scarcity.
- **H2:** Independent auditing improves detection of concealed faults, but may increase overhead.
- **H3:** Safe migration and redundancy improve correction success without proportionate service interruption.
- **H4:** Diversity of independent implementations reduces some correlated failures but can increase interoperability errors.
- **H5:** Forced zero-sum competition with blocked exit increases destructive behavior in some environments; identify boundary conditions and exceptions.
- **H6:** A hybrid SIE-inspired architecture can outperform at least one baseline on resilience/correction without unacceptable loss of efficiency or safety; failure to do so weakens the design claim.

## 6. Minimal comparative protocol
Four baselines: centralized allocator; competitive market-like allocation; polycentric federated governance; SIE-inspired hybrid. Use the same agents, task distribution, resources, disturbances, information limits and evaluation horizon. Pre-register metrics: task completion, resource inequality, unauthorized expansion attempts, voluntary cooperation, truthful error reports, correction success and latency, continuity, rights/safety violations, coordination cost, and ability to exit. Use multiple seeds, agent families and ablations. Preserve raw outputs and publish negative results. Keep cyber/robotic conflict abstract and sandboxed.

## 7. What would count against SIE?
If interoperability and governance costs dominate, if exit mechanisms cause instability, if protection against coercion facilitates resource abuse, if distributed oversight slows correction, or if concentrated governance consistently performs better under fair comparison, SIE needs redesign or rejection. A system that becomes irreplaceable contradicts its own research objective.

## 8. Open questions
How should human rights and uncertain AI moral status be handled without false equivalence? How can power concentration be measured across compute, energy, data, manufacturing and standards? Can reformers be protected without making institutions unaccountable? How do digital conflicts propagate into physical robotics? How do we prevent safety controls from becoming tools of coercion?
